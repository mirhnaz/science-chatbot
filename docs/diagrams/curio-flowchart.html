How Curio works

Curio is a science explorer for kids. You ask a question, an AI tutor explains it in a simple, exciting way, and three new questions help you keep exploring. Here is what happens behind the screen.

Using the app

From a spark to a stamp

Questions link together into a trail. Finish a trail and you earn a stamp for your collection.

  1. Pick a spark

    Home shows starter questions, called sparks. Shuffle for new ones.

  2. Follow the trail

    Five steps. Each answer offers three dive-deeper questions.

  3. Finish

    After step 5, see what you learned and share it with a grown-up.

  4. Earn a stamp

    One stamp per trail, in the topic you explored, like Space or Animals.

  5. Collect them all

    12 kinds of stamp to find. New sparks point you to topics you haven’t collected yet.

The parts

What each part does

Each part has one job. The comparisons help explain them.

On your screen

The app

A website for any computer or phone, and an app for iPad and iPhone. It shows the questions, answers and stamps.

It’s like the front desk.

Sparks

66 starter questions we wrote, across 11 science topics. Picking them needs no AI.

It’s like a deck of question cards.

Your saved stuff

Your first name, stamps and unfinished trails are saved only on your own device.

It’s like a notebook that stays in your bag.

Talking and listening

On iPad and iPhone you can say your question, and the device turns it into words itself. Any answer can be read aloud.

It’s like a helper who writes down what you say.

Backup brain

If the home computer can’t be reached, the iPad and iPhone app can use a smaller AI that runs on the device itself.

It’s like a spare tyre: slower and simpler, but it keeps you going.

Pixel animation

The moving solar system, atom and stars at the bottom are drawn by the device’s graphics chip, so the app stays smooth.

Across the internet

The secure tunnel

A service called Tailscale lets the app reach our home computer from anywhere, with everything scrambled (encrypted) on the way.

It’s like a locked pipe from your screen to our house.

At our home computer

Curio server

Our program that receives questions, checks them, adds the rulebook, and checks the AI’s reply. Written in a language called Rust.

It’s like a traffic controller and a gatekeeper.

The rulebook

The tutor instructions we wrote: explain for kids, keep it simple and exciting, around 100 words, safe ideas only, three new questions.

It’s like a teacher’s lesson plan.

Ollama and Gemma

Gemma 4 is the AI model, made by Google. Ollama is the program that runs it. We can swap in a different model without changing the app.

It’s like the brain, and the body it lives in.

Graphics card

A powerful chip, made for games, that does the AI’s maths very fast. That’s why answers take only a few seconds.

It’s like an engine.

Be ready

Questions teachers might ask

Short, true answers to the questions people usually ask about Curio.

Did you make the AI?

No. The AI is Gemma, made by Google. We built everything around it: the app, the rulebook, the sparks, the trails and stamps, and the design.

Does it search the internet for answers?

No. Gemma answers from what it learned when it was trained. Curio doesn’t look anything up online.

Is it always right?

No. AI can make mistakes. Curio checks that the reply has the right shape, not that every fact is true. That’s why the app says “AI can make mistakes. Check surprising facts with an adult.”

How do you keep it safe for kids?

The rulebook tells the AI to be kind, simple and suggest only safe ideas. The server rejects empty or very long questions and checks every reply. There are no ads and no accounts. It isn’t perfect, so ask an adult if something seems wrong.

Does it keep my questions?

Your stamps and unfinished trails stay on your own device. Our home computer doesn’t save the questions or answers. Each question is answered fresh.

Why use a home computer instead of a big AI website?

Questions stay between the device and our home, it costs nothing per question, and we learned how AI really runs. The graphics card makes it fast.

What if the home computer is off?

The website shows a friendly message to try again later. The iPad and iPhone app can switch to its backup brain, a smaller AI on the device.

Where do the sparks come from?

We wrote 66 starter questions across 11 topics, like Space, Animals, Light and the Body. Curio picks a few at random and prefers topics you haven’t earned a stamp for.

How does talking to it work?

The iPad or iPhone turns your voice into words using its own speech recogniser, right on the device. The words appear in the question box, and you tap Ask when they look right.

What languages is it written in?

The server is in Rust, the website in TypeScript, and the iPad and iPhone app in Swift. Ollama runs the AI.

Why five steps and stamps?

Five steps are enough to go deep without getting tired, and a stamp makes finishing feel like a win. Collecting all 12 kinds means exploring every part of science.

Does it remember what I asked before?

Your trail shows your earlier steps, and each new question builds on the one you picked. The AI itself doesn’t remember you between questions.
